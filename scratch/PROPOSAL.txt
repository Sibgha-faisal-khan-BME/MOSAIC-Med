MOSAIC-Med: Research Proposal
Testing Whether Multiple Small Neural Networks Can Outperform One Large Neural
Network for Healthcare Diagnosis Under Fixed Resource Budgets
Research concept: A parameter- and compute-controlled study of model multiplicity, architectural diversity, adaptive
expert routing, uncertainty-triggered computation, and robustness in medical diagnosis.
Important novelty principle: The project should not claim that ensembles, mixture-of-experts, diversity regularization, or dynamic
inference are individually new. The proposed contribution is their integration into a controlled scientific framework that asks how a fixed
neural-network capacity budget should be distributed among one large model versus multiple specialized small models, and whether
adaptive collaboration improves clinical robustness and efficiency.
1. Problem Statement
Modern medical AI often follows a scaling strategy: increase the size and capacity of a single neural network to
improve predictive performance. However, a separate line of machine-learning research has shown that splitting a
fixed parameter budget across multiple smaller models can sometimes outperform a single model with the same total
parameter count. Whether this phenomenon transfers reliably to healthcare neural networks—and under which
conditions—remains insufficiently characterized.
Healthcare provides an especially demanding setting because predictive performance is not the only requirement.
Models must also be calibrated, robust to distribution shift, resilient to noisy or missing measurements, computationally
efficient, and capable of identifying cases for which automated prediction is uncertain.
A simple comparison of 'two small networks versus one large network' would not be sufficiently novel because
ensemble learning and mixture-of-experts approaches are established. The research problem is therefore reformulated
as a controlled investigation of model multiplicity versus model scale, followed by a new adaptive framework in
which multiple compact experts develop complementary representations, are selectively activated, and can escalate
difficult cases to additional experts.
Core research question: Under an equal total parameter and computational budget, can a population of
heterogeneous small neural networks provide superior diagnostic performance, calibration, robustness, and resource
efficiency compared with a single large neural network?
2. Research Objectives
• Quantify the effect of dividing a fixed neural-network capacity budget among 1, 2, 4, 8, and potentially 16 models.
• Determine whether heterogeneous architectures provide advantages over homogeneous small-model populations.
• Develop an adaptive expert-routing mechanism that selects only the experts relevant to an individual patient.
• Introduce diversity control so that experts learn complementary rather than redundant representations.
• Develop uncertainty-triggered escalation, allowing additional experts to be activated for difficult cases.
• Evaluate robustness to noise, missing inputs, and external/distribution-shift data.
• Compare diagnostic utility against parameter count, FLOPs, latency, memory, and average number of active experts.
3. Proposed Solution: MOSAIC-Med
MOSAIC-Med (working name: Model Splitting with Adaptive, Specialized, Inhibitory and Collaborative Experts for
Medical AI) is a population-based neural architecture designed to investigate whether model diversity can substitute for
individual model scale.
The system contains multiple compact experts. Rather than forcing every expert to process every case and simply
averaging their outputs, a learned router estimates which experts are useful for a particular patient. A diversity
mechanism discourages unnecessary representational duplication, while an uncertainty mechanism can activate
additional experts when the initial prediction is unreliable.

4. Proposed Experimental Methodology
4.1 Clinical testbed
The first implementation is proposed for 12-lead ECG diagnosis using a public dataset such as PTB-XL, with an
independent external ECG dataset reserved for external validation. The same framework can later be transferred to
medical imaging or multimodal clinical data. The dataset choice should be finalized after a current literature and
data-quality review.

4.3 Expert diversity
• Homogeneous condition: all experts use the same architecture.
• Heterogeneous condition: experts use complementary architectures such as 1D CNN/ResNet, TCN, lightweight
Transformer, and frequency-domain or multi-scale temporal branches.
• Representation diversity should be measured rather than assumed.
4.4 Training objective
A conceptual objective is: L = Ldiag + λdLdiv + λrLroute + λcLcal + λfLfail.
Ldiag optimizes diagnostic prediction. Ldiv discourages harmful representation redundancy. Lroute encourages
sparse and meaningful expert selection. Lcal supports reliable probabilities. Lfail encourages complementary error
patterns. These terms should be introduced incrementally in ablation experiments rather than assuming that all terms
improve performance.
4.5 Patient-adaptive inference
Stage 1: activate a small number of experts. Stage 2: estimate predictive uncertainty and expert disagreement. Stage
3: if the case is sufficiently uncertain, activate additional experts. Stage 4: if uncertainty remains above a prespecified
threshold, abstain and flag the case for human review.

6. Statistical Analysis Plan
• Primary comparison: compare paired patient-level predictions between the large model and the proposed multi-expert
system using paired methods appropriate to each metric.
• AUROC: DeLong's test for correlated ROC curves when comparing predictions on the same test subjects.
• Binary paired outcomes: McNemar's test for paired classification disagreements at a prespecified operating threshold.
• Bootstrap confidence intervals: stratified patient-level bootstrap, preferably 2,000–10,000 resamples, for AUROC,
AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, F1, Brier score, and differences between systems.
• Multiple configurations: because 1/2/4/8/16 experts create repeated comparisons, control multiplicity using Holm
correction or a prespecified hierarchical testing procedure.
• Multiple random seeds: train each configuration across at least 5 independent seeds. Report mean ± SD and
confidence intervals across runs, while keeping patient-level test predictions separate from seed-level variability.
• Repeated-measures analysis: if many configurations and repeated seeds are compared, use a mixed-effects model
with configuration as a fixed effect and seed/dataset split as appropriate random effects; alternatively use a
preregistered non-parametric approach.
• Efficiency: compare latency/FLOPs using paired measurements where possible; report effect sizes and confidence
intervals rather than only p-values.
• Effect size: report absolute AUROC/AUPRC differences, calibration differences, relative compute reduction, and
standardized effect sizes where appropriate.
• Significance: use a prespecified α (e.g., 0.05), but interpret effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside p-values.
Statistical significance alone should not determine clinical relevance.

8. What Would Count as Evidence for the Hypothesis?
Evidence supporting the hypothesis would be a reproducible improvement of the multi-expert approach over the
parameter-matched single large network across clinically relevant metrics, with confidence intervals excluding
negligible effects, and with the advantage remaining after controlling for random seed, compute, and multiple
comparisons.
A negative result is also scientifically valuable. If increasing the number of small experts eventually hurts performance,
the experiments can identify the transition point at which individual expert capacity becomes insufficient. This prevents
the study from being designed merely to confirm the motivating claim.
9. Reproducibility and Leakage Controls
• Split at the patient level, never at the individual ECG/recording level when multiple records from the same patient exist.
• Keep the external dataset completely untouched during model development.
• Prespecify primary endpoint, primary comparison, statistical tests, and exclusion criteria before final evaluation.
• Use identical preprocessing and data augmentation rules across competing model families unless an ablation explicitly
tests them.
• Release code, configurations, random seeds, model checkpoints where licensing permits, and exact preprocessing
steps.
10. Expected Scientific Contribution
The project is designed to answer a broader question than whether an ensemble is accurate: how should a fixed
neural-network capacity budget be distributed between model scale, model multiplicity, diversity, and
adaptive computation for medical diagnosis?
A successful outcome would provide an empirical scaling curve across the number of experts, characterize when
multiplicity helps or hurts, determine whether architectural diversity contributes beyond model count, and test whether
adaptive activation can convert a many-model system into an efficient patient-specific inference mechanism.
11. Proposed Paper Structure
• Introduction — model scaling versus model multiplicity in healthcare.
• Related work — ensembles, mixture-of-experts, model splitting, diversity, uncertainty, and medical AI robustness.
• Research questions and hypotheses.

• Fixed-budget experimental protocol.
• Results: scaling with number of experts.
• Ablations: diversity, routing, inhibition, escalation, and abstention.
• Robustness and external validation.
• Statistical analysis and efficiency trade-offs.
• Discussion of when small-model multiplicity can or cannot replace individual model scale.
